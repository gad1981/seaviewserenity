Ano Syros and Ermoupoli Walking Guide: Two Towns, One Capital Island

By your Sea View Serenity hosts · Updated 2026-11-20 · ~10–12 min read

Two towns on two hills, facing each other across a few streets of rooftops: that is the heart of Syros. Ermoupoli is the 19th-century neoclassical capital, laid out around its harbour. Ano Syros is the older, Catholic hill town above it, a maze of alleys and steps. Visit Greece describes the two “twin” hills as a reminder of the two communities that have lived side by side on the island.[1] This is a walking guide that links them into one day, written for guests who stay on the west coast and drive or taxi in. We stay near Delfini Beach ourselves, and this is the day we send first-time visitors on.

Before you go

  • Start early. Both towns involve stone steps and sun. Morning and late afternoon are the best times to walk.
  • Wear proper shoes. Ano Syros is mostly navigated by marble steps and cobbles, not by road.[2]
  • Bring water and a hat. The shade in the alleys is good, but the waterfront and the summit are open.
  • Check opening hours for museums and churches, since they vary by season.

Part 1: Ermoupoli on foot (about 2 hours)

Start at the waterfront. This is where ferries dock and the town opens out in front of you. Ermoupoli stands on a natural amphitheatre of land, with neoclassical buildings, old mansions and white houses stepping down to the harbour.[2] Take a few minutes to look at the buildings along the quay before moving inland.

Walk up to Miaoulis Square. The square is the centre of the town, ringed with cafés, seating areas and palm trees, and the town hall stands there.[2] It is the best place to stop for a coffee. The official guide describes Miaoulis Square as the cultural hub of the island.[3]

Visit the Apollon Theatre. A short walk from the square, the theatre was built in 1864 by the Italian architect Pietro Sampo, inspired by Italian opera houses including La Scala, and opened with a performance of La Traviata.[4] Even if you cannot go inside, the facade is worth a pause. Check the theatre’s calendar if you would like to attend a performance.

Notice the architecture. The town’s street plan dates from 1837, and its neoclassical buildings are made of stone and marble, with arches and wooden floors, built between the 1840s and 1860s by architects from Greece, Italy and Germany.[5] Look up at balconies, cornices and doorways as you go.

Finish in Vaporia. This quarter, where sea captains traditionally lived, is known for its neoclassical mansions along narrow streets.[2] The church of Agios Nikolaos of the Rich, built in 1848 with donations from wealthy benefactors, stands here, and is the setting for the Kassiani hymns on Holy Tuesday.[6] Walk along the sea edge if the weather is calm.

Lunch break

Ermoupoli is a good place to eat well. The market streets have bakeries and sweet shops, and Visit Greece notes that Syros is known for loukoumi and halvadopita.[7] We suggest a long lunch rather than a quick stop. After the morning’s walk, you will be ready for it.

Part 2: Ano Syros (about 2 hours)

Getting there. Ano Syros is accessible by car, though the village itself is mostly a network of marble steps. The distance from the harbour up to the main entry point is around a kilometre.[2] If you do not want to climb in the heat, drive or take a taxi to the entrance, and walk down. The island’s official guide has a parking page covering Ano Syros.[8]

Walk up through the alleys. The settlement was built by the Venetians in the early 13th century, and still feels like a fortified town, with narrow roads laid out in circles and coloured doors.[2] Visit Greece describes its whitewashed walls, arches and cobbled steps leading to small, quiet neighbourhoods.[1] Take your time, and do not worry about getting lost; every route eventually leads up or down.

The cathedral of San Giorgio. The fortified cathedral dominates the summit.[1] From the top you can see across the town and the sea, and on a clear day toward neighbouring islands such as Tinos, Delos, Mykonos, Paros, Andros and Naxos.[2] Ano Syros is still the seat of the Catholic diocese of Syros and Milos.[9]

A musical stop. The island’s guide lists a museum in Ano Syros dedicated to Markos Vamvakaris, the rebetiko composer who was born here.[10] Check opening hours before you go.

Easter and festivals

If you visit around Easter, both towns are at their most atmospheric. The island’s guide notes that Syros is one of the few places where Orthodox and Catholic Easter are celebrated on the same day, with Good Friday processions, some of which end together at the town hall in Ermoupoli.[11] It is wonderful, and it is also very busy, so book ahead.

A simple one-day plan

  1. Park or arrive in Ermoupoli by 9:30 and walk the waterfront.
  2. Coffee in Miaoulis Square, then the Apollon Theatre.
  3. Wander through Vaporia and find a late lunch.
  4. Drive or taxi up to Ano Syros in the afternoon light.
  5. Walk the alleys, visit the cathedral and walk down.
  6. Return to the coast for an evening swim or a sunset dinner.

A simple loop you can finish

Harbour front → inland shopping streets → coffee → gradual climb into Ano Syros → descend before legs complain → gelato or a cold drink by the port. That loop fits a half day without heroics.[1][2]

Heat, shoes, and respect

Stone lanes reflect midday sun. Start early in July–August, carry water, and wear shoes with grip. Stay quiet in residential corners; Ano Syros is a living neighbourhood, not a theme set.

Pair the walk with a west-coast evening so the day has both capital texture and sea air.[3] If you only have one town day in the week, this is the one to protect.

Rainy shoulder-season mornings are still good walking mornings in Ermoupoli—cafés absorb the weather and the neoclassical streets take on a different mood. Save Ano Syros climbs for dry stone.
