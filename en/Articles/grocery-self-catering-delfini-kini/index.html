Self-Catering near Delfini and Kini: Groceries, Bakeries, and Villa Cooking

By your Sea View Serenity hosts · Updated 2026-11-24 · ~10–12 min read

One reason many families choose a house over a hotel room is the kitchen. On Syros, cooking some of your own meals is easy, economical and quite enjoyable, because the island has a strong food culture and a real market town. This guide explains how we shop and cook as hosts on the west coast near Delfini and Kini, and how to plan a week of self-catering without turning it into a chore.

What is in the kitchen

Our kitchen is simple and fully usable: an oven, an electric hob, a microwave, a kettle, a coffee machine, a sink and cupboards, as you can see in the photo. There is also a washing machine in the house, which helps if you want to travel with fewer clothes. Because it is a holiday kitchen, not a chef's one, we suggest bringing a favourite knife if you care about it, and checking with us if you need something specific.

Ermoupoli: the main shop

Visit Greece says Ermoupoli's market remains a lively point of reference, with small shops, family workshops and traditional confectioneries.[1] Discover Greece adds that you will find the island's cheeses, cold cuts, sweets, preserves, pastes and wild herbs in the town's delicatessens.[2] For most guests, the town is where the main shop happens, together with the supermarkets in and around it. We'll gladly tell you our current favourites when you book, because shops change and we would rather not publish names that may be out of date.

A tip that saves time: if you arrive by ferry, do a first shop in town before you drive to the house. Bring the basics (water, bread, milk, eggs, olive oil, fruit, coffee) and a cool bag if it is summer. After that, the house is a short drive away, and you won't have to think about food on your first evening.

What to buy that is distinctly Syros

Both Visit Greece and Discover Greece point to the same local specialities:

  • Cheese. San Michali PDO is a hard cow's milk cheese with a nutty, spicy flavour. Syros graviera, tyrovolia, xinotyro and kopanisti round out the cheese counter.[1] Discover Greece notes that San Michali is exclusive to the village of that name in the north of the island.[2]
  • Cold cuts. Local sausages, many flavoured with wild fennel seed and spices.[2]
  • Capers, sun-dried tomatoes and herbs. Small producers use the sun and dry climate; Discover Greece mentions wild capers from Apano Meria.[1][2]
  • Sweets. Syros loukoumi, made with the same technique for almost two centuries, and halvadopita, a halva pie with mandolato nougat between thin wafers.[1]

Allergy and preference notes

If someone in the group has severe allergies, shop yourself rather than delegating. Read labels; Greek packaging is clear once you learn a few words. For wine and spirits, decide whether Ermoupoli prices beat the convenience of a smaller coastal shop.

Run the dishwasher or sink routine nightly so ants and fruit flies never become the holiday subplot. Stone houses stay pleasant when kitchens stay reset.

Market mornings in Ermoupoli can be a cultural outing, not a chore—especially in shoulder season when stalls and bakeries feel local rather than tourist-rushed.[1][3] Then drive west with a cold bag and go swim before you put anything away except the ice cream.[2]

Bakeries, small shops and top-ups on the west coast

The west coast is quieter than the town, and that is why people like it. For daily top-ups, expect to rely on small local shops and bakeries, not a big supermarket on the doorstep. Ask us which ones are open nearby on your dates. Opening hours on Greek islands often change by season and by day. Many places close for part of the afternoon, and some are shorter on Sundays or in the off-season. Don't rely on a particular shop being open at a particular hour. Ask us, check the door, and keep an emergency plan (pasta, eggs, tinned tomatoes, olive oil).

Visit Greece describes Kini as having tavernas right on the water, so even if you don't feel like cooking, dinner is easy.[3] We usually suggest a rhythm of self-catered breakfast, a simple beach lunch from the fridge, and either a home dinner or a taverna meal, depending on the day.

A simple self-catering week

  1. Day of arrival: a big shop in Ermoupoli, then easy pasta or a salad with local cheese.
  2. Days 2–3: bakery bread and fruit for breakfast, a picnic for the beach, a taverna dinner at Kini.
  3. Mid-week: a second shop for fresh vegetables, cheese and anything you ran out of, and a longer home dinner on the terrace.
  4. Before departure: eat what is in the fridge, and leave the kitchen tidy.

Tips that make self-catering easier

  • Bring reusable shopping bags and a small cool bag.
  • Make a short list before you go. Fewer trips means more time at the beach.
  • Buy cold items last if you do a big shop in summer heat.
  • Keep olive oil, salt, pepper and a few spices on your first list, so you aren't buying them one at a time.
  • Tell us about any dietary needs before you arrive, and we'll point you to the right shops.

Cooking out of season

In winter and the shoulder seasons, a kitchen is even more useful, because some seasonal places close. Ermoupoli remains a working capital, so its shops and markets are more reliable than those in small coastal hamlets.[4] We cover that in our winter and year-round guide.

The simplest rule is to treat the island's own products as the star of the table. A good loaf, local cheese, ripe tomatoes, olive oil and fish bought that day will taste like Syros without any effort at all.
