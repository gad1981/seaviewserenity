Greece Vacation Rental Checklist: What to Verify Before You Book

By your Sea View Serenity hosts · Updated 2026-11-21 · ~10–12 min read

Before you confirm any Greek holiday rental, run through a checklist. Short-term rentals are now a mainstream part of Greek hospitality supply, especially in peak island months when hotels fill and guests look for residential-style stays.[1] Most rentals are what they say they are, but listings can hide details that matter on an island: how far the beach really is, whether the beds match the headline guest count, and what happens when something breaks. This is the list we would use ourselves, written by hosts.

1. Location: listing text vs map reality

  • Pin the property on a map and measure walking time to the beach you care about. Use satellite view to see whether the route is a path or a road.
  • Prefer precise names (“near Delfini Beach, Syros”) to vague phrases such as “Cyclades sea view”.
  • Check whether the “nearby” famous beach is the one you will use every day. Some listings name a well-known beach that is a car ride away.
  • Ask what the road is like at night and whether you need a car. On Syros, a hire car makes the island far easier.

2. Capacity and sleep layout

Headline guest counts can hide sofa-beds and extra mattresses. For families and friend groups, verify the real bedroom count, bed types and bathroom count. For example, our house has three bedrooms and two bathrooms, with four single beds and one small double, so we describe it as sleeping six comfortably. Quality-led demand is rising in Greece’s short-term rental mix, and thin, overcrowded listings underperform on guest expectations even when the nightly rate looks low.[2]

3. Kitchen, laundry, parking, Wi-Fi, cooling and heating

These decide whether a seaside week feels easy. Ask specifically about:

  • Kitchen: oven or only a hob? Fridge size? Coffee maker? Enough plates and glasses for the group?
  • Laundry: a washing machine, and somewhere to dry towels.
  • Parking: on the property, or on the street?
  • Wi-Fi: if you plan to work remotely, ask for a speed test, not only “yes”.
  • Air conditioning and heating: in high summer cooling matters, and in spring and autumn you may want heating. Do not assume.

4. Season and lead time

National tourism indicators for 2025 show a very large inbound market, with the South Aegean leading overnight volume.[3] Separately, reporting on the summer 2026 short-term rental market points to earlier booking and sharp peak-season concentration.[4] In practice, do not treat August as a last-minute market for good seaside houses. If you are flexible, late May, June and September are easier to book.

5. Legal and paperwork

  • Registration: in Greece, short-term rentals need to be registered, and a registration number should appear in the listing. If you cannot find one, ask the host.
  • Taxes and fees: check whether the quoted price includes cleaning, taxes and any local per-night charges, and what is paid on arrival. Fee rules change, so check the current position.
  • Identity: hosts may ask for passport or ID details for the registration process. That is normal.

6. Booking clarity

  1. What is included (linen, towels, cleaning, welcome information)?
  2. What is the cancellation rule, and what happens if a ferry is cancelled by weather?
  3. Who do you message on arrival day, and when do they reply?
  4. Is the listing consistent across photos, map pin, amenities and text?
  5. Is there a deposit, and how is it returned?

7. Questions worth asking the host

  • What is the wind like at the house in July and August?
  • Where is the nearest supermarket and bakery, and how far?
  • Are there any steps, steep roads or uneven surfaces to consider for elderly guests or small children?
  • What do you recommend if the beach is windy?

A good host answers these quickly and without defensiveness.

None of this is meant to make booking feel complicated. A few minutes of checking before you pay gives you a calmer week, and the hosts who welcome these questions are usually the ones who run a good stay.

Red flags to watch for

  • Very few photos, or photos that avoid showing the bedrooms, bathrooms or kitchen.
  • Vague location language with no named beach or village.
  • A guest count that does not match the beds described in the text.
  • No answer to direct questions before you book. If a host is slow to reply when they want your booking, expect slower replies once you are there.
  • Prices that look far below similar listings, with no clear reason. Check what is excluded.
  • Pressure to pay outside the platform without a clear written agreement.

None of these means a listing is bad, but each is a reason to ask more questions before you commit. A simple test is to send one specific question, such as the walking time to the beach, the bed layout or the parking arrangements, and see how clearly and quickly you get an answer. Hosts who know their property well can answer in a sentence or two, and that tells you a lot about the stay you are about to book.
