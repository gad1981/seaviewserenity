Syros Food Guide for Villa Guests: Markets, Loukoumi, and Coastal Tavernas

By your Sea View Serenity hosts · Updated 2026-11-21 · ~10–12 min read

If you have booked a house with a kitchen, food becomes part of the way you plan the holiday. Syros is a good island for this. Visit Greece’s local-products page describes a gastronomy shaped by maritime tradition and by Ermoupoli’s old commercial culture, which left the island with cheeses, cured meats and sweets that have a real identity.[1] This guide covers what to buy, what to order, and how to split your evenings between cooking and eating out.

What to put on your shopping list

  • San Michali PDO: a hard cow’s-milk cheese with a spicy, nutty character, one of Greece’s most distinctive island cheeses. Buy a wedge to grate over pasta or serve in thin slices with fruit.[1]
  • Loukoumi: the island’s signature sweet, made in flavours such as rose, mastic and bergamot, with a production tradition spanning generations.[1][2] It is the classic gift to take home, and it is lovely with Greek coffee.
  • Halvadopita: a nougat-style sweet often bought alongside loukoumi.[1]
  • Louza and local sausages: spiced cured meats that turn a simple supper of bread, tomatoes and cheese into something local.[2]
  • The everyday basics: tomatoes, cucumbers, olives, olive oil, bread, yogurt, honey and seasonal fruit from bakeries and greengrocers in Ermoupoli.

Where to shop

Do a first, larger stock-up in Ermoupoli on arrival day, either on the way from the port or as part of your first town visit. After that, bakeries and smaller shops near the coast will cover the daily bread and fresh produce. Opening hours are shorter than many visitors expect, with many smaller shops closing in the afternoon and some closing on Sundays, especially outside peak season. Buy cheese, cured meats and sweets for the week early, and check shop hours if you rely on a particular place.

Our kitchen has a large fridge with a freezer, an electric stove and oven, a microwave, a toaster and a coffee maker, so you can cook simple meals without bringing equipment. If you have a special dish in mind, such as a large roasting dish or specific pans, ask us before arrival.

Kitchen nights vs taverna nights

A good rhythm for rental guests is two or three kitchen dinners, one seafood taverna by the water, and one evening in Ermoupoli for a walk and a meal. Discover Greece’s food guide to Syros emphasises coastal seafood tavernas alongside the inland and northern flavours of the island, which is useful if you want more than beach-bar snacks.[3]

Ordering in tavernas

  • Fish is sold by weight. Ask the price per kilo and the approximate weight before you order. A whole fish can be much more expensive than the other dishes.
  • Order to share. Greek tavernas work best with several starters, a salad, one or two mains and bread for the table.
  • Ask what is local or fresh today. Menus are often long, but the daily specials are where the kitchen is proudest.
  • Check the time. Greeks eat late. In summer, kitchens are busiest from about 9 pm, so for children or an early night, book or arrive early.

Three easy villa meals

  1. Breakfast on the terrace: yogurt, honey, fruit, bread and coffee, made without leaving the house.
  2. Picnic lunch for the beach: bread, San Michali or another local cheese, tomatoes, olives, a cold bottle of water. Carry a small knife and a cool bag.
  3. Sunset dinner at home: a simple pasta with grated cheese, a tomato and cucumber salad, grilled vegetables, and louza to start.

A note on souvenirs

Loukoumi, halvadopita and local cheeses are the easiest edible souvenirs, but check airline rules for cheese and cured meat if you are flying onward, especially outside the EU. Sweets travel best. Buy them on your last day in Ermoupoli, and wrap cheese well.

Seasonal produce and what to ask for

What is on the shelf changes through the year. In summer expect tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, aubergines and stone fruit; in autumn, figs and grapes; in spring, wild greens and fresh herbs. If you are unsure what is good that week, ask at the bakery or greengrocer: shopkeepers are usually glad to recommend, and a friendly question often gets you a better choice than guesswork.

Vegetarian and other dietary needs

Greek cuisine is friendly to vegetarians, with a long tradition of vegetable dishes, beans, salads, cheeses and olive-oil-based cooking. Fasting dishes, which are meat-free and often dairy-free, are traditional around Orthodox fasting periods. If you have allergies or need to avoid specific ingredients, learn the Greek words for them or show a written note, as menus and ingredients can vary and not every kitchen is set up for allergen detail.

Coffee and breakfast

Greek coffee, frappé and filter coffee are all widely available, and the kitchen at the house has a coffee maker so you can make your own. Greek-style breakfasts usually include bread, cheese, tomatoes, olives, yogurt and honey, and with Syros’ sweets nearby, a small piece of loukoumi with a morning coffee is an easy local touch.
