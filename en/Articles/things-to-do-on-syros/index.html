Things to Do on Syros: Beaches, Ermoupoli, Food, and Easy Day Plans

By your Sea View Serenity hosts · Updated 2026-11-19 · ~10–12 min read

Syros is often called the capital of the Cyclades, and it behaves like one: it has museums, a grand theatre, a working port, a medieval hill town and a mix of beaches that range from busy and organised to wild and remote. It is also small, which means you can see a lot in a week without a rigid schedule. This is our overview of the best things to do, written by hosts whose house is a short walk from Delfini Beach, with links to our more detailed guides.

Beaches: from lively to almost empty

Visit Greece summarises the island’s beaches as a mosaic: serviced sandy shores in the south, quieter options on the west, and wilder coves in the north.[1] The most popular beaches are on the south side, protected from the meltemi. Agathopes has golden sand and shallow water that suit families, Galissas has a large bay and easy access, and Komito is calmer and more relaxed, with a windsurfing centre.[1]

On the west side, Kini combines tavernas on the water with a shallow sandy cove and a good sunset, and Delfini and Varvarousa are smaller coves nearby.[1] The island’s official guide also notes that six beaches (Agathopes, Azolimnos, Vari, Galissas, Kini and Finikas) are listed as Blue Flag beaches.[2] For a comparison of our local beaches, see the Kini, Delfini and Galissas guide.

Ermoupoli: the elegant capital

If you only spend one day away from the sea, make it Ermoupoli. Visit Greece calls it a living open-air museum, with impressive neoclassical buildings, squares and public buildings that recall a European capital.[3] Walk the waterfront, cross Miaoulis Square, visit the Apollon Theatre, and look into the Archaeological Museum, one of the oldest in Greece, which holds finds from the prehistoric settlements of Kastri and Chalandriani.[4] The Industrial Museum and the Textile Museum recall the 19th century, when Syros was a commercial and industrial centre.[4] For a step-by-step route, see our Ano Syros and Ermoupoli walking guide.

Ano Syros: the medieval hill town

On the opposite hill sits Ano Syros, the medieval settlement of the island’s Catholic community, with houses gathered around the fortified cathedral of San Giorgio.[3] Expect labyrinthine alleys, whitewashed walls, arches and cobbled steps, plus broad views of the town and the sea below. Syros is also notable for the long, peaceful coexistence of its Orthodox and Catholic communities,[3] and Easter is the most famous example, with both celebrated on the same day.[5] A museum in Ano Syros is dedicated to the rebetiko musician Markos Vamvakaris, who was born there.[4]

Food and drink

Syros has a food identity of its own, shaped by its seafaring past and the refugees who arrived in the 1820s. According to Visit Greece, the island is known for San Michali PDO cheese, Syros graviera, kopanisti, loukoumi, halvadopita, local sausages, capers and sun-dried tomatoes, and the market in Ermoupoli is still a lively point of reference.[6] Our guide to Syros food for villa guests goes into more detail, and the Kini sunset dining guide covers the waterfront. If you have a kitchen, a market morning followed by a meal on the terrace is one of the best things you can do.

Walks, villages and the wild north

Away from the coast, the official guide highlights mountain villages such as Chroussa and Episkopio, and the remote region of Apano Meria in the north, where the landscape is rugged and villages such as San Michali have stone houses and long views.[7] Visit Greece also describes northern beaches such as Grammata, reached mainly by boat from Kini, and quiet spots such as Lia, Marmari and Megas Lakkos.[1] Some are reached by paths, so bring water, sturdy shoes and check conditions locally.

Culture, music and events

Ermoupoli hosts international events such as the Classical Music Festival of the Cyclades and the Animasyros animation festival, according to Visit Greece,[3] and the island has a year-round calendar that includes summer cultural programmes, Carnival and Easter.[5] If your dates are flexible, check the municipal events listings before you book.

Outdoor activities

The official guide lists hiking, water sports, kayaking, scuba diving and nature trails among Syros’ outdoor activities, alongside wine tastings and themed tours focused on architecture, religious heritage, music or maritime history.[8] Operators and seasons vary, so contact them directly.

Day trips and island hopping

Visit Greece lists Folegandros, Tinos, Schinoussa and Sifnos among nearby escapes.[3] Syros is well connected by ferry, which makes it a convenient base for a longer Cyclades trip. For a calm stay, we suggest treating any day trip as an extra and building the week around Syros itself.

A simple way to plan

  • Two beach days at Delfini and Kini, plus one at Galissas or in the south.
  • One day in Ermoupoli for architecture, food and a long lunch.
  • One morning in Ano Syros for the alleys and the view.
  • One flexible day for the north, a museum or just the terrace.

Our seven-day itinerary puts this into a full week.

Culture without museum fatigue

Ermoupoli’s pleasure is architectural and social as much as institutional: squares, cafés, shipyard history in the air, and evening walks.[1] If you want a formal museum hour, build it into a town day; if not, you have not “failed” Syros.

Boat time and viewpoints

On calm days, a short boat hop between west-coast coves can reframe the same shoreline you already love.[2] Ano Syros gives the land version of that reframing—height, lanes, and quiet.

Food lovers should schedule one dedicated tasting stroll in Ermoupoli for loukoumi and savoury snacks, then stop collecting and go swimming again. Syros is better as a rhythm than as a checklist.[3]

Active travellers can add coastal walks and snorkelling; less active travellers can still have a full week with harbour promenades and terrace time. The island scales to energy levels better than louder Cycladic brands.
